# Intro-ask patterns

## The rule that governs all of them

The connector is doing you a favour and risking their own credibility.
Every message must make saying no cheap and saying yes lazy.

## 1. Ask to the connector

Short. Names the person and the reason. Gives an explicit out.

> Hey [name], quick one and a genuine no-problem if not.
> Are you still close with [target person] at [company]? I'm working on
> [one line, specific], and they came up as the obvious person to talk to.
> If you'd feel comfortable, I've written a paragraph below you can
> forward as is. If it's a stretch, just say so and I'll go the normal route.

Never: "can you introduce me to anyone at X". Vague asks get vague
silence. Name the person.

## 2. The forwardable blurb

Separate. Third person. Three lines. Pasteable without edits.

> [Your name] runs [role] at [company]. They're working on [specific
> thing] and wanted to compare notes on [specific topic] given [company]
> just [trigger]. Not a pitch, a 20-minute conversation.

The connector will not write this for you. If they have to, they will
not do it. Writing it for them is the entire trick.

## 3. The fallback

Assume silence. Draft the cold path for the same account, and tell the
user to send it in two weeks if no answer, without chasing the connector
twice. One ask, one reminder at most, then move on. Burning a connector
for one account is a bad trade.

## Calibration by path type

- **Strong tie, weak relevance**: ask them who the right person is
  instead of asking for the intro. Cheaper favour, better information.
- **Weak tie, strong relevance**: do not ask for an intro at all. Warm
  the tie first, or go cold and mention the mutual connection as
  context only.
- **Strong tie, strong relevance**: ask directly, and keep it to four
  lines.
